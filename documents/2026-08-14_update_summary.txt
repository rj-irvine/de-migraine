DE MIGRAINE / HEADACHE STUDY (25P01)
Update summary - 14 August 2026
Prepared by Ryan Irvine (CDS) for Paula Chu (OR)


WHAT CHANGED

- Adherence denominator corrected. Patients whose first painkiller
  prescription came more than a year after diagnosis were being dropped from
  the adherence calculation rather than counted as having no coverage. They
  were a much larger share of the control group (55%) than of the headache
  group (38%), so excluding them made controls look more adherent than they
  are. With them included, the two groups are the same.

- Prescription length is now estimated drug by drug. The data records how long
  to take the medication on only a quarter of prescriptions. Every missing one
  used to be filled in with a flat 30 days; each is now filled in with the
  typical length recorded for that same drug. The flat value was erasing the
  fact that the two groups receive different drugs, and accounted for roughly
  60% of the apparent difference in treatment coverage between them.

- Treatment length can now be reported. It previously came out as exactly 30
  days for everyone with no variation at all, which was the filled-in value
  showing through rather than a real finding. A first course of treatment now
  runs 13 days in both groups, and two thirds of first courses consist of a
  single prescription with nothing after it.

- Two competing adherence measures reduced to one. Table 4 showed adherence
  measured over each patient's own treatment period alongside adherence
  measured over a fixed year. The first gives a perfect score to anyone with a
  single prescription, which made controls appear far more adherent than
  headache patients. It has been removed; the fixed-year measure remains.

- Study period corrected to January 2021. The German data does not reach back
  to December 2016, which was carried over from the UK study. No patient is
  diagnosed before 2021. The workbook and the deck now agree. Patient counts
  are unaffected.


WHAT IT MEANS

- The main findings are unchanged. Headache patients receive far more
  painkillers, far more of it migraine-specific, have more separate courses of
  treatment (3.3 against 1.9), and a third work through four or more drugs
  against 18% of controls. These were tested against every alternative
  assumption about prescription length and did not move.

- Adherence cannot be compared between the groups, and should not be presented
  as a finding. Measured on equal terms, 1.6% of headache patients and 1.9% of
  controls have medication for at least 80% of the year. That is not evidence
  of poor adherence. It is a measure designed for drugs taken every day being
  applied to migraine drugs taken only when an attack starts. Migraine-specific
  drugs carry a treatment duration on just 3% of prescriptions for that same
  reason.


STILL OUTSTANDING

- The prescription quantity field is unusable, so total volume dispensed is not
  reported.
- Over-the-counter painkiller use is not captured anywhere, so real painkiller
  use is higher than any figure in the study.
